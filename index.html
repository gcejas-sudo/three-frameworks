Forecasting the Impacts of AI on Productivity

How input assumptions shape predictions of economic growth from AI

Preliminary Draft Stone Center on Inequality and Shaping the Future of Work

In two influential papers, both released in 2024, Daron Acemoglu and Philippe Aghion & Simon Bunel use the same formula to forecast the impact of AI on Total Factor Productivity (TFP), but they reach very different conclusions.

A comparison of their papers shows how the researchers' different input assumptions shape their forecasts.

Acemoglu (2024), Philippe Aghion & Simon Bunel (2024), and Korinek et al. (2026) use similar formulas to forecast the impact of AI on Total Factor Productivity (TFP), but they reach very different conclusions.

We created this tool to let you be part of the discussion. Change the inputs according to what you think is most relevant and determine TFP growth within the task-based model from Acemoglu (2024). As AI research advances, estimates may change. Therefore, this tool is built to be updated, and we invite you to share new studies with us.

We also present other models and estimates of TFP, Labor Productivity, and GDP Growth, demonstrating the vast array of opinions on these subjects.

A NOTE ON HARMONIZING KORINEK'S VALUES

The labels in orange are Korinek et al.'s inputs for three scenarios of AI growth. The differences with Acemoglu and Aghion & Bunel's shared definitions of values are further explained below. This section focuses on Korinek’s “First Order Approximation” Equation which yields similar cumulative results to his main model. We discuss the equation he runs the simulation on in the Other Considerations section.

Korinek et al. report a cumulative gain over six years, not an annual rate, so we divide his TFP calculation by six for two cases. We show the published figures above, which are 0.7 / 3.1 / 13.4% ÷ 6. We note that the first-order product of the four inputs would be 0.72 / 3.24 / 14.40% ÷ 6, yielding 0.12 / 0.54 / 2.40 respectively.

KORINEK ET AL.

20%/30%/50%

Modest / Substantial / Extreme

affected mass of tasks

Korinek et al. define this figure as “the fraction of the economy's tasks that AI affects, at pre-AI wage-bill weights.” The range 20%, 30%, and 50% represent the three different measures of task exposure in Eloundou et al. (2024) from least to most intense.

Key differences

Acemoglu uses task exposure while Aghion & Bunel use ability exposure. Aghion & Bunel flag occupations above the AIOE median as exposed to AI; Acemoglu uses each occupation's share of exposed tasks, then weights by wage bill to get a GDP share. Aghion & Bunel apply no weighting, assuming the GDP share of tasks exposed to AI is similar in magnitude to the share of tasks exposed to AI (despite using abilities).

In the first-order form above, Korinek does not weight the share of tasks with BLS data like Acemoglu, but defines the values as wage-bill weighted, acknowledging this as a step.

In Korinek et al.'s main specification, the share of affected tasks starts from the mid-2026 reading in all scenarios, follows a logistic path, and ends in different 2030 values depending on the scenario.

KORINEK ET AL.

20%/40%/60%

Modest / Substantial / Extreme

diffusion share of instances

Represents the percentage of task instances that AI affects. The numbers are picked by the researchers but the upper bound is informed by Bick et al. 2024 and the lower bound is informed by (Griliches 1957; David 1990; Comin and Hobijn 2010).

Key differences

Aghion & Bunel incorporate projected computing-cost declines. Acemoglu excludes them from his baseline, saying a doubling of GPU capacity “will not necessarily translate into a 50% decline in costs, in general,” citing diminishing returns, bottlenecks from other inputs, and current architectural limits.

We present Korinek et al.'s diffusion share in this section because it occupies the same place as Acemoglu's and Aghion & Bunel's share of exposed tasks profitable to automate. However, it should be noted these inputs represent different considerations. Diffusion focuses on the amount of people using AI in a task for whatever reason, whereas Acemoglu and Aghion focus on a task's profitability with AI.

We note that Korinek et al. consider diffusion at the task instance level, not the task level.

In Korinek et al.'s main specification, the diffusion starts from the mid-2026 reading in all scenarios, follows a logistic path, and ends in different 2030 values depending on the scenario.

KORINEK ET AL.

0.3/0.45/0.8

Modest / Substantial / Extreme

log unit-cost decline per instance

Represents the unit-cost decline per AI-performed instance, whether automated or augmented, also referred to as the log productivity gain per AI-performed instance.

We display it in two forms: 1.) in log points (as Korinek et al. have it in their paper) and 2.) converted increase in productivity (%).

The numbers are picked by the researchers but informed by three studies: (Brynjolfsson et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2025; Demirer et al. 2026)

Key differences

Aghion & Bunel include Peng et al., which Acemoglu excludes because he believes their setting (computer programmers on GitHub Copilot) is “less likely to be relevant to other tasks and occupations.”

We note Korinek et al.'s difference in units, as their model uses log points and not % increase in productivity.

In Korinek et al.'s main specification, the unit cost decline starts from a mid-2026 reading (0.3, 0.35, 0.45 depending on the scenario), grows in the case of the substantial and extreme scenario, and ends at 0.45 and 0.8 for these scenarios respectively.

Other Considerations

Acemoglu's variants

  • 0.53% — hard-to-learn tasks. 72.6% of exposed tasks by wage bill are easy; cost savings on the rest assumed at 7% rather than 27%. The estimate he considers most reasonable.
  • 0.66% — baseline. 4.6% of GDP impacted × 14.4% overall cost savings. He calls it an upper bound.
  • 0.89% — including Peng et al. Labor cost savings rise from 27% to 36%.
  • 0.90% — rapid compute cost decline. Svanberg et al.'s (2024) 10%-a-year scenario lifts feasibly automated tasks to 30%.

Aghion & Bunel's intervals

  • 18.5%–68% — share of GDP exposed.
  • 23%–80% — share profitable to automate.
  • 27%–40% — average cost savings.
  • 57% — labor share, fixed, taken from Acemoglu.
